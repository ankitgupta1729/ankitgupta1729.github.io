The Pigeonhole Principle: A Tiny Idea Behind Big Proofs (ISI/CMI)

One of the simplest yet most powerful tools in olympiad and ISI/CMI entrance maths. Learn to spot when 'too many objects, too few boxes' cracks a problem.

A

Ankit Gupta

Jun 21, 2026

🕳️

  • Classic examples
  • A worked ISI-style problem
  • Quick check

The pigeonhole principle sounds almost too obvious to be useful: if you put n+1n+1n+1 pigeons into nnn holes, some hole has at least two pigeons. Yet it powers some of the most elegant proofs in ISI/CMI and olympiad maths. The skill is recognising the pigeons and the holes.

The statement

  • Simple form: n+1n+1n+1 objects in nnn boxes ⇒ some box has ≥ 2 objects.
  • Generalised form: NNN objects in nnn boxes ⇒ some box has at least ⌈N/n⌉\lceil N/n \rceil⌈N/n⌉ objects.
🔑

Key idea

The entire game is choosing the boxes cleverly. The objects are usually given; your creativity goes into defining categories (remainders, pairs, regions, sums…) so that “two in one box” means exactly what you want to prove.

Classic examples

1. Same remainder. Among any 13 integers, two leave the same remainder mod 12 (boxes = 12 remainders). Their difference is divisible by 12.

2. Friends at a party. In any group of n≥2n \ge 2n≥2 people, two have the same number of friends within the group. (Boxes = possible friend-counts 0..n−10..n-10..n−1, but 000 and n−1n-1n−1 can’t both occur — so really n−1n-1n−1 boxes for nnn people.)

3. Points in a square. Place 5 points in a unit square; two are within √2⁄2 ≈ 0.707 of each other. (Divide the square into 4 smaller squares — 5 points, 4 boxes.)

✅

Tip

ISI/CMI red flag: whenever a problem says “show that there exist two … with some shared property,” try pigeonhole first. The phrase there exist two is practically a signal.

A worked ISI-style problem

Prove that among any 6 people, either 3 mutually know each other or 3 mutually don’t.

Pick one person AAA. They have 5 relationships (know / don’t know) — by pigeonhole, at least 3 are the same type, say AAA knows B,C,DB, C, DB,C,D. If any two of B,C,DB,C,DB,C,D know each other, that pair plus AAA form a “mutual-knowing” triple. If none do, then B,C,DB,C,DB,C,D are a “mutual-stranger” triple. Either way, done. (This is the famous R(3,3)=6R(3,3)=6R(3,3)=6 Ramsey result.)

Quick check

🧠

Test yourself

Pigeonhole rewards a particular kind of cleverness: inventing the right boxes. Do 15–20 varied problems and your eye for “objects vs boxes” becomes instinctive — exactly the muscle ISI/CMI tests.
